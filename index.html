Caesar Cipher

Classical · Substitution · ~100 BC

Named after Julius Caesar who used it to protect military messages. Every letter in the plaintext is replaced by a letter a fixed number of positions down the alphabet. With a shift of 3, A becomes D, B becomes E, and so on.

To decrypt, simply reverse the shift. Since there are only 25 possible shifts, an attacker can try all of them in seconds — making this historically important but cryptographically broken.

Worked Example (shift = 3)

Plain : HELLO
Cipher : KHOOR
Rule : H+3=K, E+3=H…

Vigenère Cipher

Classical · Polyalphabetic · 16th century

Blaise de Vigenère described this in 1586. It uses a keyword repeated to match the length of the message. Each letter of the keyword defines a Caesar shift for the corresponding plaintext letter — so the shift changes with every character.

This defeated frequency analysis for 300 years and was called "le chiffre indéchiffrable." Babbage and Kasiski eventually cracked it in the 1800s by finding repeated key patterns.

Worked Example (key = KEY)

Plain : HELLO
Key : KEYKE
Cipher : RIJVS
Rule : H+K=R, E+E=I…

Rail Fence Cipher

Classical · Transposition · Ancient

A transposition cipher — letters are not replaced, only rearranged. The message is written diagonally across a set of "rails" (rows), going down then up in a zigzag. The ciphertext is formed by reading each rail left to right.

Unlike substitution ciphers, frequency of letters is preserved, making it easier to identify. With only a few possible rail counts, brute force is trivial. It is historically associated with the Spartans' skytale device.

Worked Example (3 rails)

Plain : WEAREDISCOVERED
Rail1 : W . . . E . . . I . . . V . .
Rail2 : . E . R . D . S . O . E . E .
Rail3 : . . A . . . C . . . R . . . D
Cipher: WEIVERDSOEEACRD

Columnar Transposition

Classical · Transposition · 19th century

The message is written row by row into a grid whose width equals the keyword length. Columns are then read out in the alphabetical order of the keyword letters. The recipient reverses the process using the same keyword.

Used by German forces in both World Wars, often combined with other ciphers for added security. It is a true transposition — all original letters appear in the output, just rearranged.

Key order: Z(6) E(2) B(1) R(4) A(3) S(5)
